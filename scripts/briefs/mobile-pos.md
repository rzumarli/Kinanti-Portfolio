## Overview

This project focused on evaluating Mobile POS usage and adoption across physical stores, with the goal of understanding cashier behavior, identifying usability challenges, and uncovering opportunities to improve the overall experience. The research findings were translated into recommendations to improve the experience and support wider adoption across stores.

## Project Background

Mobile POS was built to help reduce queues at checkouts, especially during peak seasons such as weekends and special events when customer traffic increases. With the increase in customer traffic during peak periods, longer checkout times can put additional pressure on cashiers and negatively affect the overall shopping experience.

But months after rollout, adoption looked uneven. Some stores used it daily, others barely touched it. This led to a question: **was the existing Mobile POS actually helping cashiers do their jobs more efficiently?**

Together with Senior UX Researcher, we conducted a research study to evaluate Mobile POS UI, features, and usage process from the perspective of the store cashiers.

## Objectives

To better understand how Mobile POS is used in stores, the research focused on 3 key areas:

1. **Usage evaluation.** We want to evaluate cashiers' understanding of Mobile POS workflow, and assess how effectively the system supports their daily tasks.
2. **Problem Identification.** We want to identify what prevents cashiers from using Mobile POS effectively, including any technical, procedural, or work habits that may hinder Mobile POS adoption.
3. **Needs Assessment.** We want to understand which features support cashiers' performance, as well as their expectations, preferences, and motivations to use Mobile POS more actively.

To answer these questions, we used a combination of qualitative research methods to observe actual cashier behavior, evaluate their interaction with Mobile POS, and understand their experience and needs.

## Research Methodologies

We used three research methods to gather insights:

### 1. Field Observation

Direct observation of cashiers' behavior during in-store transactions, both with and without Mobile POS, to understand their interaction with the device, obstacles, and capture their actual workflow. However, due to low customer traffic in some stores, we were only able to observe one store during peak hours.

### 2. Think-Aloud Usability Testing

Cashiers demonstrating the payment process (core scenario) using Mobile POS while verbalizing their thoughts throughout the process. This helped us understand what they found intuitive, where they encountered obstacles, and what they looked for to complete the task.

### 3. In-Depth Interview

Conducted one-on-one interviews with cashiers to dig deeper on their personal experience, motivation, preference, perception, challenges, and needs related to Mobile POS features and workflows.

With these three methods, we gathered insights from both observed behavior and direct feedback from the cashiers.

## Findings

After doing the research, we found that **one store was using Mobile POS daily, meanwhile the other didn't use Mobile POS at all**. How come?

From the research, we understand the general journey of users. Numbers of users **dropped on the transition between consideration on using Mobile POS and actually choosing to use Mobile POS**. What could possibly affected the decision?

We combined the insights from 3 methods, and we found that there are **three crucial points** that are affecting Mobile POS usage decision:

**1. Store area & till capacity**

The two stores differed a lot in size, one was nearly 3 times larger than the other with two tills versus one. Their average sales were close, with the larger store slightly ahead.

The larger store felt it could handle customer traffics with its existing tills, so staffs feels that Mobile POS are unnecessary. In contrast, the smaller store had only one tills, limited space, and long queues, so it relied on Mobile POS to keep customers moving.

**2. Team availability**

When the store got busy, the retail assistants were needed on the floor to help customers across different sections. As a result, most prioritized serving customers over operating Mobile POS.

Additionally, only a subset of staff was authorized to handle transactions, limiting the number of staff available to user Mobile POS during peak hours.

**3. Mobile POS till area**

Mobile POS still needs a physical station. Payment confirmation happens on the secondary device, and bill printing uses the same printer as the main cashier. The station also has to sit near the packaging area, since purchase still have to be bagged for customers.

Setting up a separate till for Mobile POS felt like more work than it saved.

## Recommendations

Back to where it started, was Mobile POS actually helping cashiers? Our answer is, **in the store where it was needed, yes**. In the other, the problem is more on the surrounding factor. Low adoption came from store size, staffing, and physical setup rather than the quality of the tool itself.

So our recommendations cover two areas. **People & Process** covers staff availability, work habits, and store readiness. **System & UI/UX** covers the product's own limitations and where it can improve.

### Process & People

**1. Store prioritizing with data** *(responds to: Store area & till capacity)*

Instead of using Mobile POS the same way everywhere, we encouraged client to categorize stores using quantitative data: number of tills, sales area, and sales. Each falls into three categories:

- **Priority**: stores with limited tills, long queue, and small area.
- **Potential**: stores that could benefit with Mobile POS, but may need more encouragement and exercise.
- **Non-priority**: stores that are not in need of Mobile POS.

This way, efforts goes where the Mobile POS really solves the problem, and support matches what each stores actually needs.

**2. Plan staff availability for peak hours** *(responds to: Team availability)*

Make sure the number of authorized staff on peak shift matches the number of available tills, **plus one more** if the store wants to open Mobile POS till. Review shifts composition ahead of peak seasons, and brief the team on how tasks are split when the store uses Mobile POS.

**3. Design the Mobile POS till area** *(responds to: Mobile POS till area)*

Provide a detailed technical setup guide, includes where the items are packed, the bill printer, and the secondary device to confirm payment. Include a flexible cashier space that fits one more person. For potential stores, simulate the layout and queue flow before they open a new till.

**4. Practice, simulation, and deliverable checklists** *(for stores new to Mobile POS)*

Have area managers run simulations per area and provide checklist to guide Mobile POS setup and ongoing use. Priority stores receive direct support, meanwhile Potential stores can be nurtured gradually.

### System & UI/UX

**5. Make payment flow seamless** *(responds to: Mobile POS till area)*

The ideal idea is that Mobile POS can keep customer data stays integrated, confirm payment automatically, and send the bill digitally by default. Customers who still want a physical copy can request one, which can be printed on the bill printer. This removes the dependency of additional devices for most transactions, so the Mobile POS can actually be mobile.

## Where this stands

These recommendations haven't been put into practice yet, so I can't claim an outcome. What I can say is, this study changes the way I think about the problem. **A product can be well designed and still go unused when the environment doesn't support it**. If the recommendation moves forward, I would want to see adoption tracked by store category (priority, potential, and non-priority) rather than just one overall number. Because a low number on non-priority store is not a failure.
