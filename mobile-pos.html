UX Research · Case Study

Mobile POS Usage Evaluation

Evaluating how store cashiers actually use Mobile POS, why adoption differed so much between stores, and what would help it spread. A UX research case study for an outdoor retail company.

Role
UI/UX Designer & Researcher
Users
Store cashiers
Methods
Field observation · Think-aloud usability testing · In-depth interviews
Note
Under NDA, the brand name is omitted

Overview

This project focused on evaluating Mobile POS usage and adoption across physical stores, with the goal of understanding cashier behavior, identifying usability challenges, and uncovering opportunities to improve the overall experience. The findings were translated into recommendations to improve the experience and support wider adoption across stores. My contribution covered research support, interview moderation, research synthesis, usability testing, UX analysis, and the UX recommendations, working together with a Senior UX Researcher.

Background

Mobile POS was built to help reduce queues at checkouts, especially during peak seasons such as weekends and special events, when longer checkout times put pressure on cashiers and hurt the shopping experience. But months after rollout, adoption looked uneven. Some stores used it daily, others barely touched it. That led to one question: was the existing Mobile POS actually helping cashiers do their jobs more efficiently?

Objectives

  1. Usage evaluation. Evaluate cashiers' understanding of the Mobile POS workflow, and assess how effectively the system supports their daily tasks.
  2. Problem identification. Identify what prevents cashiers from using Mobile POS effectively, including technical, procedural, or work-habit factors that hinder adoption.
  3. Needs assessment. Understand which features support cashiers' performance, along with their expectations, preferences, and motivations to use Mobile POS more actively.

Research methods

1 · Field observation

Direct observation of cashiers' behavior during in-store transactions, with and without Mobile POS, to understand their interaction with the device, the obstacles, and their actual workflow. Due to low customer traffic in some stores, only one store could be observed during peak hours.

2 · Think-aloud usability testing

Cashiers demonstrated the payment process, the core scenario, using Mobile POS while verbalizing their thoughts. This showed what they found intuitive, where they hit obstacles, and what they looked for to complete the task.

3 · In-depth interviews

One-on-one interviews with cashiers from two stores, one with high adoption and one with low adoption at similar revenue, to dig into personal experience, motivation, preference, perception, challenges, and needs.

Findings

After the research, one thing stood out: one store was using Mobile POS daily, while the other did not use it at all. Mapping the general user journey, the number of users dropped at the transition between considering Mobile POS and actually choosing to use it. Combining the insights from all three methods, three crucial points affected that decision:

1 · Store area & till capacity

The two stores differed a lot in size, one was nearly 3 times larger than the other, with two tills versus one, while their average sales were close. The larger store felt its existing tills could handle the traffic, so staff felt Mobile POS was unnecessary. The smaller store, with one till, limited space, and long queues, relied on Mobile POS to keep customers moving.

2 · Team availability

When the store got busy, retail assistants were needed on the floor across different sections, so most prioritized serving customers over operating Mobile POS. Only a subset of staff was authorized to handle transactions, which limited who could run Mobile POS during peak hours.

3 · The Mobile POS till area

Mobile POS still needs a physical station. Payment confirmation happens on a secondary device, bill printing uses the same printer as the main cashier, and the station has to sit near the packaging area because purchases still need to be bagged. Setting up a separate till felt like more work than it saved.

Recommendations

Was Mobile POS actually helping cashiers? In the store where it was needed, yes. In the other, the problem sat in the surrounding factors: low adoption came from store size, staffing, and physical setup rather than the quality of the tool itself. The recommendations therefore cover two areas.

Process & people

  1. Store prioritizing with data. Categorize stores using tills, sales area, and sales data: Priority (limited tills, long queues, small area), Potential (could benefit, needs encouragement and practice), and Non-priority (not in need). Effort goes where Mobile POS really solves the problem.
  2. Plan staff availability for peak hours. Match the number of authorized staff on peak shifts to the number of tills, plus one more if the store opens a Mobile POS till. Review shift composition ahead of peak seasons and brief the team on task splitting.
  3. Design the Mobile POS till area. Provide a detailed technical setup guide covering packing space, the bill printer, and the secondary device, with a flexible cashier space that fits one more person. For potential stores, simulate the layout and queue flow first.
  4. Practice, simulation, and checklists. Area managers run simulations per area and provide checklists for setup and ongoing use. Priority stores receive direct support; potential stores are nurtured gradually.

System & UI/UX

  1. Make the payment flow seamless. Ideally Mobile POS keeps customer data integrated, confirms payment automatically, and sends the bill digitally by default, with a printed copy on request. Removing the dependency on additional devices lets Mobile POS actually be mobile.

Where this stands

These recommendations have not been put into practice yet, so no outcome can be claimed. What the study changed is how the problem is framed: a product can be well designed and still go unused when the environment does not support it. If the recommendations move forward, adoption should be tracked by store category (priority, potential, non-priority) rather than one overall number, because a low number in a non-priority store is not a failure.
